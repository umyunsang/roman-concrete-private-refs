                                                                                                             1. INTRODUCTION
                                                                                                                 In caementiciis autem structuris primum est de harena quaerendum, ut ea sit idonea ad materiem miscendam . . .
                                                                                                                 In concrete structures one must first inquire into the sand so that it will be suitable for mixing into
                                                                                                                 mortar . . .
                                                                                                                                                                                         Vitruvius (1931, 2.4.1), ca. 25 BCE


                                                                                                             The concrete of ancient Roman construction, fabricated through a vast expanse of Roman ter-
                                                                                                             ritories during the late Republican and Imperial eras, second century BCE to third century CE,
                                                                                                             has maintained cohesion and structural functionality for two millennia (Delaine 2024). A mor-
                                                                                                             tar mixture (materia) prepared with lime calcined from limestone in kilns at 850–900°C, reactive
                                                                                                             aggregate(s) mainly as volcanic tephra or ceramics, and freshwater or seawater binds the con-
                                                                                                             crete, in which cobble-sized coarse aggregate clasts of hewn volcanic or carbonate rock and/or
                                                                                                             ceramic fragments (caementa) form a reinforcing framework (Figure 1). Marcus Vitruvius Pollio
                                                                                                             (ca. 90–20 BCE) described these materials in his treatise, The Ten Books on Architecture.
                                                                                                                 The binding processes in these aggregate structures, or caementiciae structurae, rely on fluid in-
                                                                                                             teractions with reactive aggregates to activate initial cohesion and sustain resilience at long ages.
                                                                                                             The reactive aggregates are principally scoriaceous volcanic tephra, or excavated sands (harenae
                                                                                                             fossiciae), for buildings in the Roman region and pumiceous volcanic tephra with a large fine-ash
                                                                                                             fraction, or dust (pulvis), for buildings surrounding the Gulf of Naples and massive marine con-
                                                                                                             crete harbor installations (Vitruvius 1931, 2.4.1, 2.6.1–2.6.2, 5.12.2) (Figures 1 and 2). Enormous
                                                                                                             volumes of pulvis were eventually transported through the Mediterranean region to build concrete
                                                                                                             structures on land and in the sea (Figure 3).
                                                                                                                 By late first century BCE, builders had refined selections of volcanic tuff for dimension stone
                                                                                                             masonry and the coarse aggregate framework of concrete, as well as tephra aggregate for binding
                                                                                                             mortars. Roman natural scientists recorded empirical observations of these materials and stated
                                                                                                             hypotheses for early reaction processes and long-term resilience (solidatis) in caementiciae structurae,
                                                                                                             described in Naturales quaestiones (Seneca 1971, ca. 4 BCE–65 CE), de Architectura (Vitruvius 1931,
                                                                                                             ca. 90–20 BCE), and Naturalis historia (Pliny the Elder 1962, 23/24–79 CE). Long-term precip-
                                                                                                             itation of silicate mineral cements toughens the concrete in bridges, cisterns, aqueducts, marine
                                                                                                             harbor structures and fish-raising tanks, and in architectural monuments, which have maintained

                                                                                                                                       Portland cement is made by sintering limestone and aluminosilicate materials at 1,450°C to

                                                                                                                                   addition. The chemical reaction of this powder with water (Marchon & Flatt 2016) produces

                                                                                                                                   modern concrete. The English word cement comes from the Old French ciment, derived from the
                                                                                                                                   Latin caementum, or hewn stone, perhaps referring to limestone as a source for lime (CaO). The
                                                                                                                                   English word concrete comes from the Latin concretum, meaning solidified, compact, or con-
                                                                                                                                   densed, derived from concrescere, to grow together [con- (together) and crescere (to grow)]. No
                                                                                                                                   direct equivalents of the English terms cement and concrete, however, appear in Roman texts.
                                                                                                                                   Furthermore, no cement product was employed in the ancient structures.
                                                                                                                                       This review integrates ancient texts, archaeological observations, and analytical descriptions
                                                                                                                                   of the geochemical, mineralogical, and material characteristics of well-preserved Roman concrete
                                                                                                                                   structures to provide new insights into the relevance of caementiciae structurae to modern concrete
                                                                                                                                   construction. Several fundamental questions guide ongoing research:

                                                                                                                                          tures, caementiciae structurae, that form marvels of architectural brilliance and sustainable

                                                                                                                                          through the Mediterranean region reflect Roman builders’ adherence to these guiding

                                                                                                       2. GEOLOGIC COMPONENTS: MATERIA, CAEMENTA, CALYX
                                                                                                       Ancient Roman concrete contains approximately 70–75 vol% caementa in terrestrial structures
                                                                                                       and 40–45 vol% caementa in marine structures, on average; the binding mortar (materia) has an
                                                                                                       approximately 1:2.7 vol% ratio of hydrated lime to reactive aggregate (Samuelli Ferretti 1995,
                                                                                                       Brandon et al. 2014, Delaine 2021). Rock (or ceramic) aggregates thus constitute 85–90 vol% of

                                                                                                       the ancient structures. A stiff mix was packed into forms, not poured, with caementa compacted
                                                                                                       in the mortar (Oleson et al. 2006). This produces a clast-supported, conglomeratic concrete fab-
                                                                                                       ric (Figure 1g,i,j) commonly hidden from view by incertum and reticulatum facings during the
                                                                                                       late Republican era (Figure 1d) and brick facings, testaceum, mainly during the Imperial era
                                                                                                       (Figure 1c,h). There is great diversity in formwork for construction ranging from immense vaulted
                                                                                                       ceilings in architectural structures to reef-like marine structures in the sea (Brandon et al. 2014,
                                                                                                       Lancaster 2015).

                                                                                                       2.1. A Long Fascination with Roman Architectural Concrete
                                                                                                       Architects and natural scientists of the Renaissance such as Leon Battista Alberti [1988 (1452)],
                                                                                                       the Age of Enlightenment such as Barthélémy Faujas de Saint-Fond (1780), and the nineteenth
                                                                                                       century including Faustino Corsi Romano [1991 (1828)] and August Choisy (1873) described Ro-
                                                                                                       man building stones and concrete, including diverse sources of volcanic pozzolan by Faujas de
                                                                                                       Saint-Fond (1780) (Thiéry 2024). Fascination with Roman construction materials grew during
                                                                                                       the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as scholars explored monumental architecture
                                                                                                       and ruins in Rome and the volcanic rocks of its surroundings (Supplemental Figure 1a). In re-
                                                                                                       search supported by the Carnegie Institution, H.S. Washington (1906) employed geochemical and
                                                                                                       mineralogical analyses to describe the potassic nature of the Roman Comagmatic Province, and
                                                                                                       E.B. Van Deman (1912a,b) made systematic, empirical descriptions of the aggregate components
                                                                                                       of the architectural concrete. T. Frank (1924) inferred the provenance of Republican era construc-
                                                                                                       tion materials. Further hypotheses for sources of these materials and building technologies were
                                                                                                       developed by G. Lugli (1957) and M.E. Blake (1947, 1959, 1973), yet they were not integrated
                                                                                                       with contemporaneous studies of the stratigraphy of the Monti Sabatini and Colli Albani volcanic
                                                                                                       districts (Scherillo 1944–1946, Fornaseri et al. 1963).
                                                                                                           The publication of La geologia di Roma, Il centro storico (Funiciello 1995) created a stratigraphic,
                                                                                                       hydrologic, seismologic, and geotechnical foundation for identifying sources of the construction
                                                                                                       materials of the ancient monuments and their responses to an exceptionally dynamic geologic
                                                                                                       environment. Studies of the Quaternary fluvial-volcanic stratigraphy and geochronology of the
                                                                                                       Capitoline Hill distinguished pyroclastic deposits filling separate paleovalleys; these deposits
                                                                                                       record the responses of the Tiber River to sea level change in the nearby Tyrrhenian Sea (Alvarez
                                                                                                       et al. 1996). 40 Ar/39 Ar age dating of the potassic volcanic deposits led to a formalized volcanic
                                                                                                       stratigraphy calibrated to sea level rise and fall over the past 800 ka (Karner et al. 2001) (see the
                                                                                                       sidebar titled Volcaniclastic Rock in Ancient and Modern Concrete).

                                                                                                       2.2. Volcanic Foundations of the Ancient Concrete
                                                                                                       Roman concrete construction has its roots in explosive pyroclastic eruptions of the Monti Saba-
                                                                                                       tini and Colli Albani volcanic districts, which underlay Rome and its surroundings, and the
                                                                                                       Campi Flegrei and Somma Vesuvius volcanic districts, which surround the Gulf of Naples (Italy)

                                                                                                       Tephra and tuff are pyroclastic deposits produced by explosive eruptions (pyro = fire + clastic = broken in pieces).
                                                                                                       Tephra is composed of fragments: glass quenched from molten rock, crystals, and host rocks. Tuff is the rock pro-
                                                                                                       duced by lithification of tephra, mainly through growth of mineral cements. Tufa, or travertine, is calcium carbonate
                                                                                                       rock deposited by thermal springs and lakes. These deposits occur throughout the Roman region, and they surround
                                                                                                       the Gulf of Naples.
                                                                                                           Romans used tephra in its original particle size distribution as mortar aggregate. The coarse ash- and lapilli-sized

                                                                                                       hydrated lime and forms part of a complex cementing matrix (Figure 4). In modern concrete, volcanic rock pozzolan
                                                                                                       is ground to less than 100 µm to replace cement. The aggregate is inert sand and gravel, 0.5–4.75 mm.

                                                                                                       They also show resistance to chemical attack. In marine structures, however, a stiff fracture response produces cracks
                                                                                                       and seawater ingress that corrodes steel reinforcement. A Roman analog marine concrete may be a self-sustaining,
                                                                                                       highly resilient alternative.

                                                                                                       2.3. A Hydrated Lime Activator
                                                                                                       Volcanic glass, zeolite minerals, opal, and poorly crystalline clay minerals are natural pozzolans,
                                                                                                       derived from the modern city of Pozzuoli, ancient Puteoli, a maritime transport hub for pulvis
                                                                                                       (Figure 3a; Supplemental Figure 1b). Pozzolans react with hydrated lime, or calcium hydrox-
                                                                                                       ide [Ca(OH)2 ], to produce durable cementing hydrates (Massazza 1998, Mertens et al. 2009,
                                                                                                       Elsen et al. 2022). In Roman concrete, the volcanic glass and mineral components of tephra
                                                                                                       (Figure 2) react pozzolanically with hydrated lime to produce a poorly crystalline calcium-
                                                                                                       aluminum-silicate-hydrate (C-A-S-H) binding phase that bonds tephra particles within a complex
                                                                                                       mortar (Figure 4). Myriad methods for quicklime hydration and mixing with reactive aggre-
                                                                                                       gates (Traini 2013, Copsey 2019) are recorded by the microstructural features of Roman mortars,
                                                                                                       demonstrated in part through mortar and concrete replicas and reproductions (e.g., Oleson et al.
                                                                                                       2006, Goldsworthy & Zhu 2009, Brandon et al. 2014, Jackson et al. 2014). Silicate cementi-
                                                                                                       tious hydrates, rather than calcium carbonate phases, dominate mortars accessed by drill cores
                                                                                                       in thick walls or underwater structures (e.g., Jackson et al. 2013b, 2014, 2017; Brandon et al. 2014,
                                                                                                       pp. 166–75; Rispoli et al. 2020).

                                                                                                       stability field, pH 8–10 (de’Gennaro et al. 2000, Sheppard & Hay 2001). The compositions of
                                                                                                       phillipsite and silica-enriched Al-tobermorite crystals are similar to mineral cements that crys-
                                                                                                       tallize in young oceanic basaltic tephra at Surtsey volcano, Iceland ( Jakobsson & Moore 1986,
                                                                                                       Montesano et al. 2023, Jackson et al. 2024b) (Supplemental Figure 3). They indicate that the
                                                                                                       chemical and mineralogical system evolved from pozzolanic reactions with hydrated lime to
                                                                                                       post-pozzolanic reactions with alkaline pore fluids, produced by slow infiltration and reaction
                                                                                                       of seawater within the massive concrete structures ( Jackson et al. 2017).

                                                                                                       4.1. A Diaspora of Pumiceous Tephra, Pulvis
                                                                                                       Chemical analyses of pumice clasts in the mortars of the marine concretes provide clues to pyro-
                                                                                                       clastic source deposits. Trachytic glass, primary volcanic crystals, mainly sanidine, and authigenic
                                                                                                       minerals, mainly phillipsite, are reactive components in the ancient mortars (Figure 2d,e). Pumice
                                                                                                       clasts separated from the mortars should therefore record depletion in major elements relative to




Downloaded from www.annualreviews.org. Guest (guest) IP: 70.66.248.217 On: Sat, 01 Aug 2026 03:15:49
                                                                                                       source compositions. Indeed, compositions of pumice clasts from drill cores of concrete harbor
                                                                                                       structures (Figure 3c) show lower SiO2 and Na2 O + K2 O in the total alkali-silica diagram, relative
                                                                                                       to compositions from Campi Flegrei deposits (Brandon et al. 2014); these are elements involved
                                                                                                       in authigenic precipitation of phillipsite, for example (Figure 2e). Rare earth elements (REE) are
                                                                                                       thought to remain immobile in glass and crystals. When analyses of REE in pumice clasts sepa-
                                                                                                       rated from the marine mortars are compared with the same elements in Campi Flegrei pyroclastic
                                                                                                       deposits and coupled with mineralogical data, a close correlation is shown for the harbor concrete
                                                                                                       structures (Brandon et al. 2014, pp. 148–59).
                                                                                                           Pumiceous tephra aggregate occurs in Republican era concrete structures less than 5 km from
                                                                                                       deposits near Baia (Figure 3b): the Thermal Complex of Baia, second century BCE, Piscina
                                                                                                       Mirabilis, ca. 27 BCE–14 CE (Figure 1d), and harbor structures at Baianus Lacunis, Baianus Si-
                                                                                                       nus (Figure 1e), first century BCE, and Portus Iulius, 35 BCE (Brandon et al. 2014, pp. 260–65;
                                                                                                       Rispoli et al. 2019a,b, 2020). Approximately 20,000 m3 of pumiceous tephra and tuff caementa were
                                                                                                       transported ∼2,400 km to Caesarea Maritima at Sebastos, Israel, ca. 23–15 BCE (Figure 1f,g). Tens
                                                                                                       of thousands of kilos of pumiceous tephra were also shipped to marine structures along the west
                                                                                                       coast of the Italian peninsula (Figure 3b,c; Supplemental Figure 2, Supplemental Table 1). The
                                                                                                       vaulted concrete architecture of monuments in Rome also contains pumiceous tephra from the
                                                                                                       Gulf of Naples volcanic districts (Marra et al. 2013), as do coastal structures in Sardinia, northern
                                                                                                       Africa, Aquilea, and the lagoon of Venice (Djerad et al. 2022; Dilaria et al. 2023a,b, 2024; Fort
                                                                                                       et al. 2024).

                                                                                                       4.2. Ses Llumetes Shipwreck: Insights into Aggregate Transport
                                                                                                       The Ses Llumetes shipwreck, first century CE, at 2 m depth in the Balearic Islands near Porto

                                                                                                       7a,b). Bulk compositions of the shipwrecked pumice fall within the compositional field of Baia
                                                                                                       tephra deposits, although glass analyses show more dispersion (Figure 3c). REE of a single lapillus

                                                                                                       Recent research verifies some of the empirical observations and hypotheses for mechanisms that
                                                                                                       underlie resilience of caementiciae structurae recorded by Roman natural scientists (Figure 8) and
                                                                                                       reveals the chemical, mineralogical, and mechanical mechanisms underlying the longevity of the

                                                                                                       ancient concrete (Figure 9).

                                                                                                       5.1. Roman Hypotheses: Energetic Inputs and Hydration Dynamics
                                                                                                       Producing the reactive materials of marine and architectural concrete structures requires intensive
                                                                                                       energetic input (Vitruvius 1931, 2.5.1–2.5.3, 2.6.1–2.6.3) (Figure 8, labeled 1). Fire that gener-
                                                                                                       ates calcination of limestone in kilns, which produces quicklime (calyx), and the Fire element that
                                                                                                       generates hydrothermal activity and explosive pyroclastic eruptions, which produce pumiceous
                                                                                                       tephra (pulvis), tuff (tofus), and sponge-stone pumice (spongia) aggregates, are hypothesized to
                                                                                                       create materials depleted in the Liquid element (Vitruvius 1931, 2.6.2–2.6.3). (This hypothesis
                                                                                                       refers to Four Elements theory, described in Vitruvius 1931, 2.2.1.) Through hypothesized accep-
                                                                                                       tance of the Liquid element, the mixture hydrated with (sea)water is observed to quickly cohere
                                                                                                       and solidify (solidantur) (Figure 8, labeled 2). It grows hot with hypothesized latent heat (latenti
                                                                                                       calore), produced by a hot lack of moisture (calida umoris) suddenly being filled with water (aqua)
                                                                                                       (Vitruvius 1931, 2.6.4). Perhaps Roman thought considered a potential lying inherent, or latent,
                                                                                                       within a substance that could be realized or released with application of certain empirical proto-
                                                                                                       cols, such as adding seawater to lime and pulvis. In the sense of this latent potential for reactivity,
                                                                                                       with consequent change in free energy of the system, the physical manifestation would be a reac-
                                                                                                       tion product and evolution of heat (L.W. Hobbs, personal communication 2025). A calculation of
                                                                                                       the temperature rise in the Baianus Sinus pila associated with exothermic, or latent, heat released
                                                                                                       mainly through formation of C-A-S-H binding phase finds that elevated temperatures, 85–95°C,
                                                                                                       persist in the center of the pila for 1–2 years ( Jackson et al. 2013a).
                                                                                                           Extended hydration (Figure 8, labeled 3) is implied for marine concrete (Vitruvius 1931, 2.6.1)
                                                                                                       and hypothesized for architectural concretes that “maintain themselves in their [own] moisture
                                                                                                       (umore) into very old age (ad summam vetustatem)” (Vitruvius 1931, 2.8.6). This fluid, also described
                                                                                                       as vital sap or juice (sucum), must remain in the mortar (materia) to maintain cohesion in the con-
                                                                                                       crete wall (Vitruvius 1931, 2.8.2; Brandon et al. 2014, p. 20). Pliny the Elder (1962), 100 years
                                                                                                       later, described the marine concrete as “a single stone mass (lapidem unum) impregnable to the
                                                                                                       waves and every day stronger” (35.166) (Figure 8, labeled 4), validating the implication of ex-
                                                                                                       tended hydration hypothesized by Vitruvius and possibly foreshadowing the diagenetic processes
                                                                                                       that produce zeolite cements in volcaniclastic rocks (Passaglia et al. 1990). The natural consol-
                                                                                                       idation of puteolanus pulvis in the presence of water (si aquam attigit) to become rock (saxum) is
                                                                                                       also described by Seneca (1971, 3.20.3–3.20.4). Young oceanic basaltic tephra at Surtsey volcano,
                                                                                                       Iceland, lithifies to form tuff with Al-tobermorite and phillipsite mineral cements at 30–140°C
                                                                                                       ( Jakobsson & Moore 1986, Montesano et al. 2023, Jackson et al. 2024b).

                                                                                                                                  5.3. Processes of Transformational Toughening
                                                                                                                                  Transformation toughness refers to an increase in the fracture toughness of a material at the tip
                                                                                                                                  of an advancing crack, which is the direct result of a stress-induced phase transformation that
                                                                                                                                  absorbs energy and resists crack propagation. Zirconia, for example, can undergo a phase trans-
                                                                                                                                  formation from tetragonal to monoclinic structure under stress, effectively absorbing the energy
                                                                                                                                  that would otherwise cause the crack to further propagate (Hannink et al. 2010). The resilience
                                                                                                                                  of caementiciae structurae may derive from an analogous process, which does not involve a direct
                                                                                                                                  phase transformation but instead involves the transformational effect of a cementitious fabric

                                                                                                       that evolves in complexity over time (W.M. Kriven, personal communication 2025). Intricate as-
                                                                                                       semblages of cementing hydrates in microstructures that derive, in part, from the heterogeneous
                                                                                                       permeability structure of the concrete (Figures 4, 5c, 6, and 9) may mechanically interfere with
                                                                                                       crack propagation. These obstacles may cause deflection at the tip of an advancing crack, increas-
                                                                                                       ing fracture energy required to create new crack surface area, and further toughening the concrete
                                                                                                       through extending its ability to absorb energy and plastically deform before fracturing (van Mier
                                                                                                       1997, Bažant et al. 2009). Arc-bending test experiments of a Markets of Trajan mortar replica

                                                                                                       6. ANCIENT PRINCIPLES FOR MODERN SELF-SUSTAINING
                                                                                                       CONCRETE
                                                                                                       6.1. Responses of Concrete Structures to External Influences
                                                                                                       The caementiciae structurae of extant monuments in Rome have resisted structural-scale failure
                                                                                                       during at least six episodes of moment magnitude 6.7–7 ground shaking events associated with
                                                                                                       slip on Appennine fault systems 80–130 km to the northeast (Frepoli et al. 2010, Galli & Molin
                                                                                                       2014), as well as potential chemical decay associated with repeated inundations with Tiber River
                                                                                                       floodwaters (Aldrete 2007). The concrete structures contain common macroscale fractures, with
                                                                                                       rough surfaces that link by complex segment overlap and bridging ( Jackson et al. 2014), yet the
                                                                                                       monuments maintain structural functionality as residences, museums, offices, churches, and aque-
                                                                                                       ducts. Sectional failure of the Colosseum, 70–90 CE, Baths of Caracalla, ca. 215 CE, and Basilica
                                                                                                       of Maxentius, ca. 313 CE, for example, mainly occurred in Late Antiquity or the Middle Ages
                                                                                                       through subsurface instabilities; problematic structural design; removal of marble and travertine
                                                                                                       dimension stone, columns, and cladding; and absence of regular maintenance (Funiciello et al.
                                                                                                       1995, Samuelli Ferretti 2005, Galli & Molin 2014).
                                                                                                            The capacity of the architectural concretes to fracture at macroscale but remain intact at struc-
                                                                                                       tural scale results from long-term interactions between architectural form and material responses
                                                                                                       to static loads and seismic ground shaking (e.g., Brune & Perucchio 2012). For example, a series of
                                                                                                       concrete lateral arches connect the 8.6-m span of the central vault of the Great Hall of the Markets
                                                                                                       of Trajan to the surrounding superstructure (Figure 1h); the elevated location of the arches was
                                                                                                       perhaps intended to mitigate both static and seismic loadings. Selections of silt- to gravel-sized
                                                                                                       Pozzolane Rosse scoriaceous tephra (Figure 2a–c; Supplemental Figure 1a) and cobble-sized
                                                                                                       tuff (and ceramic) caementa as reactive aggregates are important components of the chemical and
                                                                                                       mechanical toughening mechanisms that reinforce these structures (Figures 1i and 4). An initial
                                                                                                       pozzolanic system evolved to post-pozzolanic processes, which created further reinforcements
                                                                                                       as strätlingite and Al-tobermorite mineral cements (Figures 4e,f and 9s) and remodeled poorly
                                                                                                       crystalline phases (Figures 4c,d and 9r) ( Jackson et al. 2009, 2014, 2024a; Seymour et al. 2021).
                                                                                                       Slow permeation of Tiber River floodwater into walls and foundations may have benefited these
                                                                                                       processes (Figure 6a,c).
                                                                                                            The caementiciae structurae of extant marine harbors in the Bay of Pozzuoli have absorbed en-
                                                                                                       ergy from Md < 4 seismic ground shaking associated with activity at Campi Flegrei caldera, as well
                                                                                                       as seafloor displacements produced by secular subsidence superimposed on periods of ground up-
                                                                                                       lift (Costa et al. 2022, Orsi 2022); current uplift and subsidence phases have an axisymmetric shape
                                                                                                       centered in Pozzuoli harbor (Pino et al. 2025). Pliny the Elder (1962) described inundation of the
                                                                                                       sea, as floods, and subsidence of the land during earthquake activity, as well as the sudden motion
                                                                                                       of waves in the sea (2.82, 2.83, 2.86; Hughes 2013). The Caesarea Maritima harbor (Figure 1f,g),

                                                                                                                                                                www.annualreviews.org • Caementiciae Structurae   289
                                                                                                             for example, was subjected to strong wave forces associated with distant earthquakes in first cen-
                                                                                                             tury CE and sixth century CE, as well as long-term subsidence of the seafloor (Reinhardt et al.
                                                                                                             2006, Galili et al. 2021).
                                                                                                                 The force of impact of strong waves on concrete structures, generated by typhoons,
                                                                                                             hurricanes, winter storms, and earthquakes over the centuries, could be close to tens of thou-
                                                                                                             sands of metric tons per square meter in large events (Mehta 1991, p. 25). Strong wave action
                                                                                                             affects structures mainly in the supratidal zone, where the concrete is also vulnerable to abrasion
                                                                                                             by sand and gravel suspended in seawater. Maritime concretes are now systematically reinforced
                                                                                                             with steel but may suffer pitting corrosion of steel by chlorides, salt weathering, disaggregation,

                                                                                                             and spalling (Mehta 1991, pp. 64–9; Hren et al. 2024). The reinforced structure must therefore
                                                                                                             remain highly impermeable to seawater inflow. High-performance blended cement concrete with
                                                                                                             supplemental cementitious materials has very low permeability, 10−17 –10−18 m2 (Figure 6e), yet
                                                                                                             steel-reinforced structures are vulnerable to corrosion through fracture and inflow of seawater
                                                                                                             (Mohammed et al. 2003).
                                                                                                                 By contrast, ancient Roman designs for concrete structures in diverse hydrological environ-
                                                                                                             ments produce silicate cementitious phases through beneficial interactions of fluids with reactive
                                                                                                             aggregates, which reinforce cohesion over a long history of service life. In Caesarea Maritima ma-
                                                                                                             rine concrete, poorly crystalline magnesian aluminosilicate binding phases, M-(A)-S-H, form from
                                                                                                             pore solutions derived from combustion residues of organic materials, dolomitic rock, and shell
                                                                                                             fragments (Secco et al. 2019, 2022); there are also disordered low-calcium C-A-S-H phases. In a
                                                                                                             first-century CE well-cistern in the lagoon of Venice, early calcium-bearing binding phases are
                                                                                                             also replaced by M-(A)-S-H phases (Dilaria et al. 2024). In the Piscina Mirabilis, the cistern con-
                                                                                                             structed near Miseno, Italy, as the terminal reservoir of the Aqua Augusta, Al-tobermorite crystals
                                                                                                             in micropores derive from long curing of concrete containing trachytic tuff and ceramic reactive
                                                                                                             aggregates (Rispoli et al. 2020) (Figure 1d; Supplemental Figures 1b and 3). Mortars of the
                                                                                                             Aqua Virgo aqueduct, ca. 19 BCE, near Rome contain blade-like cementitious gels (Calzolari et al.
                                                                                                             2023). The mortar of the Aqua Traiana aqueduct, 109 CE (Figure 9t), records C-(N,K-)A-S-H
                                                                                                             gel with an acicular phase as well as authigenic zeolite in scoria vesicles (Medeghini et al. 2024).
                                                                                                             And 11 Å tobermorite and 14 Å piomberite occur in the mortar of Ponte Augusto at Narni, 27 BCE
                                                                                                             (Cantisani et al. 2002). Slow infiltration of fluids into the reactive fabric of these concretes ev-
                                                                                                             idently renews cohesion and, perhaps, restores structural functionality to a more resilient state
                                                                                                             than that of the early-age concrete.
                                                                                                                 The average measured uniaxial compressive strength, 6 MPa, and Young’s modulus, 7,320 GPa,
                                                                                                             of 9-cm diameter drill core specimens of the marine concrete are similar to that of Imperial era
                                                                                                             conglomeratic concrete in the monuments of Rome, 6 MPa and 2,560 MPa, respectively, measured
                                                                                                             in 1 m3 reproductions with ceramic caementa (Samuelli Ferretti 1995; Brandon et al. 2014, pp. 174–
                                                                                                             80). Typical uniaxial compressive strength of ordinary Portland cement concrete is about 30 MPa,
                                                                                                             and Young’s modulus of hardened paste is 10–30 GPa.
                                                                                                                 Roman designs did not require high compressive strength for structural functionality in broad
                                                                                                             reefs, breakwaters, moles, and wharves in the sea—or in massive walls, piers, and substructures
                                                                                                             on land. The principal performance requirements of the ancient marine structures were to resist
                                                                                                             the impact force of strong waves and remain anchored on the seafloor, to maintain resistance
                                                                                                             to abrasion in the supratidal zone, to preserve cohesion during moderate magnitude earthquake
                                                                                                             ground shaking, and to gain in resilience over time so that “neither the force of the waves nor
                                                                                                             the effect of water can dissolve them” (Vitruvius 1931, 2.6.1). Laboratory measurements of low
                                                                                                             compressive strength do not adequately represent the extraordinary mechanical resilience of the
                                                                                                             ancient structures.

                                                                                                       6.2. Marine and Coastal Concrete Infrastructure
                                                                                                       Roman builders integrated material resilience, beneficial hydrologic interactions with seawater,
                                                                                                       ecological compatibility, and geomorphological continuity to maintain the structural function-
                                                                                                       ality of caementiciae structurae in marine environments for two millennia. The concrete mix was
                                                                                                       formulated with seawater to deliberately enhance chemical compatibility with the marine envi-
                                                                                                       ronment, as well as to conserve freshwater resources. Infiltration of seawater through the complex
                                                                                                       permeability structure of the concrete (Figure 6b,d) produces chemical interactions with reactive
                                                                                                       pumiceous aggregate (Figure 2d–f ) that renew cohesion through long-term growth of silicate




Downloaded from www.annualreviews.org. Guest (guest) IP: 70.66.248.217 On: Sat, 01 Aug 2026 03:15:49
                                                                                                       mineral cements (Figures 4g–i and 9j–q). The external surfaces of submerged structures have 10–
                                                                                                       20-cm-thick marine biological and mineralogical encrustations, including algae, seaweed, sponges,
                                                                                                       worm casts, and carbonate minerals, which create a protective surface and symbiotically enhance
                                                                                                       infrastructural resilience (Brandon et al. 2014, pp. 8, 40, 275, 277). The structures were commonly
                                                                                                       built as reefs that follow the natural topography of the seafloor and parallel coastal structures de-
                                                                                                       rived from longshore sediment transport, thus maintaining geomorphological continuity over the
                                                                                                       centuries.
                                                                                                           A framework of conglomeratic rock and ceramic aggregate(s) reinforces the massive struc-
                                                                                                       tures. Wood formwork may continue a short distance below the seabed, yet the relatively fragile
                                                                                                       planks were likely not intended to anchor the large and heavy masses (Brandon et al. 2014,
                                                                                                       pp. 189–222). The ductile response measured in Roman analog marine concrete arc-bending
                                                                                                       tests (Figure 5d) suggests that the structures may resist fracture more effectively than Port-
                                                                                                       land cement concrete, which has substantially greater early compressive strength yet typically
                                                                                                       degrades monotonically until maintenance and/or replacement, with greater rates of decrease
                                                                                                       caused by cracking, seawater ingress, and corrosion of steel reinforcement (e.g., Torres-Acosta
                                                                                                       et al. 2007). Cohesion of extant Roman marine structures remains robust, yet eventual disag-
                                                                                                       gregation would return the conglomeratic reinforcing framework to the seafloor as benign rock
                                                                                                       sediment.


                                                                                                       7. FUTURE STUDIES AND APPLICATIONS
                                                                                                       An inferred modern production process for Roman concrete would emit as much greenhouse gas
                                                                                                       per volume as conventional cement-based concrete (Martinez et al. 2025). This high initial en-
                                                                                                       ergy demand, recognized by Romans (Figure 8), and its negative environmental impacts could
                                                                                                       be mitigated by increased durability and longevity, requiring less maintenance and replacement
                                                                                                       over time. Nevertheless, direct adoption of caementiciae structurae in conventional concrete de-
                                                                                                       signs would be impeded by slow early strength gain, pore fluid pH < 12.5 insufficient to protect
                                                                                                       steel reinforcement from corrosion, and low compressive strength that requires large structural
                                                                                                       elements (Martinez et al. 2025).
                                                                                                          Concrete structures are critical to the repair of damaged shorelines, aging waterways, and mit-
                                                                                                       igation of rapid hydrological change associated with sea level rise and climate instabilities, storm
                                                                                                       surge, and coastal erosion (US Army Corps of Engineers 2021). Well-integrated saltwater con-
                                                                                                       crete structures would ideally produce long-lasting coastline reinforcements that create favorable
                                                                                                       environments for plant, invertebrate, and fish communities (Suedel et al. 2022). This is in contrast
                                                                                                       to hard, armored, conventional Portland cement concrete structures that disrupt coastal ecosys-
                                                                                                       tems and require costly maintenance and/or replacement, due in part to corrosion of embedded
                                                                                                       steel reinforcement; innovations in seawater-mixed Portland cement concretes currently show
                                                                                                       unpredictable durability outcomes (Aghajanzadeh et al. 2026).
                                                                                                          Elements of Roman material design for caementiciae structurae could provide a paradigm
                                                                                                       to be incorporated in modern materials for shoreline concretes. Reef-like structural designs

                                                                                                                                                                www.annualreviews.org • Caementiciae Structurae   291
                                                                                                             (Brandon et al. 2014, pp. 122–40; De Graauw 2022) would balance material tensile capacity with
                                                                                                             structural dimensions to achieve an effective safety factor; large coarse aggregate, or caementa,
                                                                                                             would increase toughness and provide a self-reinforcing framework without steel or glass fiber
                                                                                                             rebar; and dynamic chemical interactions among reactive aggregates and pore fluids would ben-
                                                                                                             eficially remodel and toughen cementitious fabrics. Fracture could induce a beneficial change
                                                                                                             to material permeability (e.g., Heap & Kennedy 2016) as fluid ingress interacts with reactive
                                                                                                             aggregates to generate cementitious products that repair and restore the material continuum.
                                                                                                             Absence of steel– or glass fiber reinforced polymer–reinforcement would reduce initial ma-
                                                                                                             terial fabrication costs and emissions, as well as construction costs, corrosion, and ecological




Downloaded from www.annualreviews.org. Guest (guest) IP: 70.66.248.217 On: Sat, 01 Aug 2026 03:15:49
                                                                                                             end-of-life costs (Younis et al. 2018, Morales et al. 2021). Viable sources of reactive volcanic
                                                                                                             rock and synthetic glass foam aggregates for large-scale structures currently remain elusive,
                                                                                                             however.
                                                                                                                 Insights gained through further archaeometric investigations of ancient mortars and con-
                                                                                                             crete would clarify interactions among the material characteristics of reactive aggregate materials,
                                                                                                             environmental conditions, and time that influence dynamic evolution of post-pozzolanic cemen-
                                                                                                             titious hydrates and microstructures, as well as the interactive material and mechanical behavior
                                                                                                             of specific Roman structures. These studies could further inform the resilience of novel lime-clay
                                                                                                             binding systems, for example, in which a pumice addition mitigates damaging alkali silica reaction
                                                                                                             through crystallization of calcium-bearing minerals in vesicles, which then evolve to phillipsite and
                                                                                                             chabazite zeolite (Tahsin et al. 2025), similar to the post-pozzolanic processes of caementiciae struc-
                                                                                                             turae. Reactions of synthetic calcium (alumino)silicate glasses, basaltic glasses, borosilicate glasses,
                                                                                                             and recycled domestic glass with aqueous solutions at varying pH (e.g., Snellings 2015, Jantzen
                                                                                                             et al. 2017, Malferrari et al. 2022) have produced various hydrated, crystalline precipitates, includ-
                                                                                                             ing Al-tobermorite. The cross-linked structure and Al3+ substitutions for Si4+ , balanced by Ca2+ ,
                                                                                                             Na+ , and K+ cations in the interlayer of Roman syntheses of Al-tobermorite crystals ( Jackson
                                                                                                             et al. 2013a,b, 2017), could provide clues to creating low-temperature crystallization pathways for
                                                                                                             cation change (e.g., Komarneni et al. 1987) in alkali-activated concretes for cementitious barriers
                                                                                                             and waste encapsulations.

                                                                                                             8. SUMMARY
                                                                                                             Advanced analytical methods and materials testing validate Roman hypotheses for mechanisms of
                                                                                                             self-sustaining resilience in the caementiciae structurae of ancient architectural and marine construc-
                                                                                                             tion. An early pozzolanic system activated with hydrated lime consolidates and strengthens the
                                                                                                             concrete, mainly through production of C-A-S-H binding phase in a complex cementing matrix.
                                                                                                             Low-temperature interactions of reactive volcanic rock aggregates with pore fluids then generate a
                                                                                                             post-pozzolanic, alkali-activated system that toughens the concrete through production of silicate
                                                                                                             mineral cements and, in some structures, remodeling of the early binding fabric. A heterogeneous
                                                                                                             permeability structure, derived in part from cobble-sized caementa and a broad size distribution
                                                                                                             of reactive mortar aggregates, principally potassic scoriaceous tephra and trachytic pumiceous
                                                                                                             tephra, facilitates these processes. A survey of pulvis pumiceous tephra employed in marine and
                                                                                                             coastal concrete structures, first century BCE to third century CE, and the Ses Llumetes ship-
                                                                                                             wreck reveal a broad diaspora of tephra aggregate transported from the Campi Flegrei volcanic
                                                                                                             district to structures throughout the Mediterranean region. Incorporation of elements of Roman
                                                                                                             material design for caementiciae structurae in modern materials with reactive rock aggregates for
                                                                                                             shoreline and coastal concrete structures could improve structural functionality through beneficial
                                                                                                             hydrologic interactions with saltwater, self-sustaining material resilience, ecological compatibility,
                                                                                                             and continuity with coastline geomorphology.
